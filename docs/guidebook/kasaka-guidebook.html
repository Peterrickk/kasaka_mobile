How to use this guidebook

Your SakaSense device measures four things in your soil: pH (sourness), Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P) and Potassium (K). This book tells you whether each number is right for your crop, and what to add if it is not.

  1. Take a reading the right way (page 4). A bad sample gives a wrong answer.
  2. Open your crop's chapter. Kangkong p. 8, Pechay p. 12, Sweet potato p. 16, Okra p. 20.
  3. Find each number in the reading table. Every number falls in one of four boxes: ✕ LOW ! SLIGHTLY LOW ✓ GOOD ▲ HIGH
  4. Read the code. The device screen and the Kasaka app show a 4-digit code, like 7210. Look it up in the crop's Fix-it page or the code finder on page 27.
  5. Do the fix, then test again 2 weeks later. Write it down in the record sheet (page 27).

Amounts in this book

All amounts are given for one bed of 1 m × 20 m (20 m²), the standard bed size of the Department of Agriculture vegetable guides. The amount per hectare is shown beside it. One 50-kg bag per hectare equals 100 g per bed. No scale? Use the spoon and cup table on page 25.

Please read

The SakaSense probe gives a quick field estimate, not a laboratory test. Use it to decide what to do this week. For a new field, or once a year, bring a soil sample to the DA Regional Field Office 8 Regional Soils Laboratory or ask your City/Municipal Agriculture Office about the Mobile Soil Laboratory (page 26). If a lab report and your probe disagree, follow the lab.

Always follow the label and safety instructions on fertilizer bags.

Where the numbers come from

Soil conditions in Leyte: Bureau of Soils and Water Management (BSWM) Soil Health Assessment maps, made with DA-RFO 8 and LGUs. Crop pH, planting and fertilizer practice: DA Regional Field Unit and Agricultural Training Institute production guides, and Visayas State University (VSU) research in Leyte. Full list on page 28.

Taking a good soil reading

One reading from one spot can mislead you. Soil changes from one corner of a bed to the next, so always take five readings per bed and use the average.

  1. Pick 5 spots in a zig-zag (W shape) across the bed. Stay away from the edges, compost piles and places where fertilizer was just put.
  2. Clear the surface. Push away grass, mulch and stones.
  3. Make sure the soil is moist, not dry and not flooded. If it is dry, water the spot and wait 30 minutes.
  4. Push the probe in to the root zone (about 10–15 cm deep). The metal parts must touch the soil fully, with no air gaps.
  5. Press Measure on the device or in the Kasaka app and keep the probe still until the numbers stop changing (up to 45 seconds).
  6. Write down pH, N, P and K. Do the same at the other 4 spots, then add each number up and divide by 5.
  7. Wipe the probe clean with clean water and a cloth. Store it dry.

When to test

  • 3 weeks before planting, so there is time to lime.
  • Mid-season, 2 weeks after side-dressing.
  • After harvest, to plan the next crop.

Do not test

  • within 7 days after fertilizing
  • within 3–4 weeks after liming
  • in waterlogged soil, or right after heavy rain

The device may also show temperature and humidity. These are for your records only; the soil advice in this book uses pH, N, P and K.

pH is the key that unlocks food in the soil. When soil is too sour, the fertilizer you apply gets locked up and the plant cannot use it, so you waste money. Fix pH first, then fertilize.

What this means for your farm

  • Most soils have a good pH (5.6–6.8), but about 1 in 5 farms is sour enough to need lime. Around Tacloban, Palo and Santa Fe the maps show mostly good pH with some strongly acidic patches.
  • Phosphorus is the most common shortage. Almost 4 in 10 farms are low or moderately low. Low-P patches are common between Tanauan, Tabontabon, Dagami and Tolosa.
  • Potassium is usually enough. 6 in 10 farms rate high, so many farmers can use less potash. Test first before buying.
  • The very high-pH (alkaline) soils are mostly on limestone in western Leyte, not around Tacloban.

Every farm is different, so these are averages, not your result. Your own SakaSense reading tells you about your bed. Southern Leyte farms tested by the DA RFO 8 Mobile Soil Laboratory in 2026 were also found slightly to highly acidic.

Quick choices

  • pH between 5.0 and 5.4 and no money for lime? Plant sweet potato. It tolerates sour soil best of the four.
  • Low spot that stays wet? Plant kangkong. Pechay, okra and sweet potato all suffer in waterlogged soil.
  • Need cash fast? Pechay and kangkong are ready in about a month.
  • Low nitrogen and little money for fertilizer? Sweet potato needs the least nitrogen. Too much N actually makes small roots.
  • Rotate. Do not plant pechay in the same bed season after season; it invites club root. Follow it with sweet potato or okra.

Tacloban weather: rain falls all year, heaviest from November to January, and typhoons come often. Use raised beds (15–20 cm) and drainage canals for pechay and okra during the wet months. Sweet potato vines lie low and recover well after strong winds.

Codes72xx
Good pH
5.5 – 7.0
First harvest
3–4 weeks
Spacing
20 × 20 cm

Growing kangkong

  1. Soil. Grows in most soils; best in sandy loam with good drainage and lots of organic matter.
  2. Prepare the land. Plow and harrow 2–3 times, one week apart, 15–20 cm deep. Make beds 1 m wide × 20 m long. Mix in the basal fertilizer (page 10). Plastic mulch is optional.
  3. Plant. Sow 2–3 seeds per hill in 4 rows per bed, 20 cm between rows and between hills. Stem cuttings (25–30 cm, 2–3 nodes in the soil) also work. Kangkong can also be grown in containers.
  4. Water right after planting and every day after. Kangkong never wants to dry out.
  5. Weed often in the first 3 weeks, until the plants cover the bed.
  6. Harvest 3–4 weeks after planting by cutting the shoots about 5 cm above the soil, or pull whole plants. Cut plants regrow; cut again every 2–3 weeks and feed after each cut.

Pests and diseases

Caterpillars, whiteflies and aphids are the common pests. Keep the bed clean, remove weeds and damaged plants, and check under the leaves twice a week. Use resistant varieties and biological or chemical control only when needed, following the label.

Source: DA RFU VII Guide on Vegetable Production / ATI-VII Kangkong Production Guide (2020); BPI Kangkong Production Guide.

P and K boxes follow the BSWM soil ratings used for Leyte: "high" P starts at about 15 mg/kg (Olsen) and "high" K at about 75 mg/kg. Kangkong is a leafy crop, so it needs a good supply of nitrogen, but an economic-rate study on acid soils found it responds little to potassium.

Pechay

Brassica rapa L. · petsay
Codes71xx
Good pH
5.5 – 7.0
Harvest
3–4 wk after transplant
Spacing
10 × 20 cm

Growing pechay

  1. Soil. Sandy to clay loam with plenty of organic matter. Pechay grows best at pH 6.0–6.5.
  2. Prepare the land. Plow and harrow 2–3 times, one week apart, 15–20 cm deep. Beds 1 m × 20 m. Mix in basal fertilizer (page 14).
  3. Raise seedlings in a raised seedbed (1 m × 10 m, 15 cm high, fine sterilized soil) or in seedling trays with 2–3 seeds per hole. Cover lightly and sprinkle with water.
  4. Seedling care. Shade from strong sun and heavy rain. Water morning or afternoon. Harden for one week before transplanting by giving more sun each day.
  5. Transplant 2–3 weeks after sowing, in the late afternoon, 10 cm between plants and 20 cm between rows. Water right away and then daily.
  6. Harvest 3–4 weeks after transplanting, early morning or late afternoon. Cut with a sharp knife or pull with the roots and wash.

Pests and diseases

Pests: aphids, flea beetles and diamondback moth. Diseases: powdery mildew on seedlings, bacterial soft rot, and club root (swollen, knotted roots; plants wilt at midday). Pull and bury sick plants. Club root is worse in sour soil, so keeping pH near 6.5 and rotating crops helps prevent it.

Source: DA RFU VII Guide on Vegetable Production / ATI-VII Pechay Production Guide (2020); DA Caraga pechay techno guide (pH 5.5–6.5).

Sweet potato

Ipomoea batatas (L.) Lam. · kamote
Codes23xx
Good pH
5.5 – 6.8
Harvest
3–4 months
Spacing
75 × 25 cm

Growing sweet potato

  1. Soil. Loose, well-drained sandy loam. It tolerates sour, poor soil better than the other three crops, but not standing water.
  2. Planting material. Use healthy tip cuttings 25–30 cm long from vigorous, pest-free plants. Clean planting material of improved varieties is available from VSU PhilRootcrops in Baybay City and DA RFO 8.
  3. Make ridges 75 cm apart and 25–30 cm high. Ridges keep roots dry in the rainy season.
  4. Plant one cutting per hill, 25 cm apart, with 2–3 nodes buried, slanting into the ridge.
  5. Weed and hill up at about 2–4 weeks and again at 1 month, pulling soil back up the ridge. Cover any soil cracks to keep weevils out.
  6. Lift the vines once a month so they do not root at the nodes.
  7. Harvest 3–4 months after planting for modern varieties (traditional varieties take up to 6 months).

Pests and diseases

The worst pest is the sweet potato weevil, which bores into the roots and makes them bitter. Prevent it by using clean cuttings, hilling up to close soil cracks, harvesting on time, and removing old roots and vines after harvest. Rotate with a non-Ipomoea crop; kangkong is a relative of kamote, so do not rotate kamote with kangkong.

Source: VSU research in Leyte (Asio et al., Tropentag 2018: 0.75 × 0.25 m spacing, ridges); DA RFO 02 Sweet Potato Production Guide (2012); PhilRootcrops, VSU.

Why these numbers?

The recommended rate there was 40-40-60 kg N-P₂O₅-K₂O per ha; phosphorus above 40 kg gave no gain. Kamote takes up more potassium than nitrogen, so its "good" K box is the highest of the four crops.

Okra

Abelmoschus esculentus (L.) Moench
Codes31xx
Good pH
5.5 – 7.0
First pods
40–75 days
Spacing
20 × 25 cm

Growing okra

  1. Soil. Grows in any soil but best in sandy loam, pH 5.5–7.0 (best 6.0–6.8). Choose a high-yielding, pest-resistant variety that buyers in your market like.
  2. Prepare the land. Plow and harrow 2–3 times, one week apart, 15–20 cm deep. Beds 1 m × 20 m. Mix in basal fertilizer (page 22). Plastic mulch is optional.
  3. Soak seeds in clean water overnight. Sow right away, or air-dry first.
  4. Sow 2–3 seeds per hill, 1 cm deep, 20 cm between hills and 25 cm between rows. Replant missing hills right away. Thin to 2 plants per hill 15 days after planting.
  5. Water whenever the soil is dry or plants wilt.
  6. Hill up one month after the plants come up, covering the base of the plants. Hand-pull the weeds between plants.
  7. Harvest young, tender pods 7–10 cm (3–4 inches) long, 4–6 days after the flower opens. Pick every 2–3 days; old pods get tough and slow down the plant.

Pests and diseases

Pests: cotton stainer and stink bugs. Diseases: cercospora leaf blight, powdery mildew, fruit rot and root-knot nematode. Uproot and bury sick plants, keep the field clean, and rotate. Root-knot nematodes build up when okra follows okra.

Source: DA RFU VII Guide on Vegetable Production / ATI-VII Okra Production Guide (2020); BPI Okra Production Guide; DA RFO 02 Okra Production Guide.

  • pH 6.2 and K 150 → ✓ GOOD
  • N 32 → ! SLIGHTLY LOW
  • P 14 → ! SLIGHTLY LOW

Two nutrients are short, so the code is 3120 (several low). Add the extra 14-14-14 at basal and test again 2 weeks after the seedlings come up.

Why these numbers?

pH 5.5–7.0 from the DA RFU VII/ATI-VII and BPI okra guides. Okra flowers and fruits for many weeks, so it needs steady nitrogen and good phosphorus for pods. Since phosphorus is the most common shortage in Leyte soils (page 6), okra growers should watch the P row closely.

Soil careAcidic and alkaline soil

Fixing sour (acidic) and alkaline soil

Lime is not fertilizer. It does not feed the plant; it unlocks the food already in the soil and the fertilizer you add later. Many Leyte soils are sour because of heavy rain and years of chemical fertilizer.

How much lime?

Your pH readingPer bed (20 m²)Per hectare
! SLIGHTLY LOW (a bit sour)2 kg (about 7 cups)1 ton
✕ LOW (too sour)4 kg (about 13 cups)2 tons
Too sour, heavy clay soilup to 6 kgup to 3 tons

Sandy soils need less lime than clay soils. These are starting amounts that raise pH by roughly half to one unit. A DA soil lab test gives the exact lime requirement for your field.

How to lime

  1. Use agricultural lime (ground limestone) or dolomite. Dolomite also adds magnesium.
  2. Spread it evenly over the bed 2–4 weeks before planting.
  3. Mix it into the top 15 cm of soil and water it in. Lime only works when it is mixed with moist soil.
  4. Wait at least 2 weeks before applying urea or ammonium fertilizer. Lime and urea together lose nitrogen to the air.
  5. Test pH again after 3–4 weeks. Do not lime again within the same season unless the reading is still low.

Alkaline soil (pH too high)

  • Do not add lime, dolomite or wood ash.
  • Work in compost or well-rotted manure every season.
  • Use ammonium sulfate 21-0-0 as your nitrogen source; it slowly lowers pH. Use 2 g of ammonium sulfate for every 1 g urea in the plan.

Soil careCompost and lab testing

Compost and the DA soil laboratory

Feed the soil, not only the plant

Soil tests in Leyte and Southern Leyte keep finding low organic matter and sour soil, linked to years of chemical-only fertilizing. Municipal agriculture officers in Region VIII recommend locally available organic amendments such as poultry manure and rice hull.

  • Add 1–2 sacks of compost or manure per bed every season. It holds water and fertilizer so less is washed away by the rain.
  • Rice-hull ash and carbonized rice hull loosen clay and add some potassium.
  • Use well-rotted manure only. Fresh manure burns roots.
  • Mulch with rice straw to protect the soil from heavy rain.

Getting a laboratory soil test

The SakaSense probe is for quick checks. A lab test is more complete (it includes organic matter and the exact lime requirement). Get one for every new field and once a year.

  1. Take soil from 10–15 spots in a zig-zag across the field, 0–20 cm deep, using a clean spade.
  2. Mix it all in a clean pail. Take about 1 kg of the mixed soil.
  3. Dry it in the shade (not in the sun) and remove stones and roots.
  4. Put it in a clean plastic bag and label it: your name, barangay, field, crop planned, date.
  5. Bring it to the DA RFO 8 Regional Soils Laboratory, or ask your City/Municipal Agriculture Office about the DA Mobile Soil Laboratory schedule.
